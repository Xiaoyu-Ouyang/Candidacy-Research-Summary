\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Simulating nonequilibrium quantum dynamics is difficult because the full time-evolved wavefunction can develop rapidly growing entanglement, making standard state-based tensor-network methods expensive at long times.
In many applications, however, the desired output is not the full wavefunction but a local observable, amplitude, response function, or reduced influence functional.
This suggests a different computational goal: instead of compressing the full state, one should compress only the information that remains relevant to the final spacetime tensor-network contraction.

This report develops and tests an environment-dependent message-propagation approach for this purpose.
The central object is a temporal matrix product state message arising from transverse contraction of a spacetime tensor network.
Conventional density-matrix compression truncates each message as an isolated object, whereas the reduced-transition-matrix compression studied here chooses the message subspace using the opposite boundary or reverse message as its contraction environment.
This makes the compression criterion closer to the target amplitude or local observable.
Numerical tests in one-dimensional finite and infinite systems, finite and infinite tree lattices, and infinite two-dimensional belief-propagation approximations show that environment-dependent compression typically delays the onset of systematic errors at fixed bond dimension.
The improvement is strongest in one-dimensional transverse contractions and remains useful on tree and two-dimensional message-passing problems, although convergence of the nonlinear message updates and loop corrections in two dimensions remain important open issues.
Overall, the results support treating quantum-dynamical messages as contraction-dependent objects rather than standalone states, providing a practical route toward longer-time classical simulations of local quantum dynamics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Background}
\subsection{Overview of equilibrium and non-equilibrium many-body physics}
Quantum many-body physics asks how collective phenomena emerge from many interacting quantum degrees of freedom.
Although the microscopic equation \(H\ket{\Psi(t)}=i\hbar\partial_t\ket{\Psi(t)}\) is compact, a generic state of \(N\) local degrees of freedom with local dimension \(d\) requires \(d^N\) amplitudes, making exact classical representation infeasible.
For equilibrium problems, many successful methods avoid this worst-case scaling by exploiting physical structure: Hartree-Fock and coupled cluster approximate molecular ground states \cite{roothaanNewDevelopmentsMolecular1951,bartlettCoupledclusterTheoryQuantum2007}, while DMRG and related tensor-network methods exploit entanglement area laws in low-energy local systems \cite{whiteDensityMatrixFormulation1992b,eisertColloquiumAreaLaws2010}.

Nonequilibrium real-time dynamics is harder because the desired quantities are often time-dependent observables, spectra, transport coefficients, or relaxation processes rather than only ground-state energies.
Exact diagonalization, Krylov methods, and Chebyshev expansions \cite{AnalysisKrylovSubspace, hochbruckKrylovSubspaceApproximations1997, tal-ezerAccurateEfficientScheme1984} apply \(e^{-iHt}\) to a state without forming the exponential, but still operate in the full Hilbert space.
Other approaches avoid a full wavefunction trajectory by choosing reduced dynamical objects: linear response and Green's functions target correlation functions \cite{fetterQuantumTheoryManyparticle2003}; TDDFT propagates the density with approximate exchange-correlation functionals \cite{rungeDensityFunctionalTheoryTimeDependent1984}; sparse Pauli dynamics evolves selected Heisenberg-picture Pauli strings \cite{rallSimulationQubitQuantum2019,begusicFastConvergedClassical2024a,begusicRealTimeOperatorEvolution2025,rudolphPauliPropagationComputational2025}; and open-system methods propagate reduced density matrices or influence functionals after separating a subsystem from its environment \cite{breuerTheoryOpenQuantum2002,nakajimaQuantumTheoryTransport1958a,zwanzigEnsembleMethodTheory1960,goriniCompletelyPositiveDynamical1976,lindbladGeneratorsQuantumDynamical1976a,tanimuraTimeEvolutionQuantum1989,tanimuraNumericallyExactApproach2020,feynmanTheoryGeneralQuantum1963,makriNumericalPathIntegral1995}.
These methods show a common strategy: replace the full state by the smallest object that still contains the information needed for the target observable.

\subsection{Current tensor network methods for quantum dynamics}
Tensor-network time-evolution methods compress a state, operator, or path-integral object using entanglement and correlation structure \cite{orusPracticalIntroductionTensor2014a,paeckelTimeevolutionMethodsMatrixproduct2019}.
In one dimension, TEBD, time-dependent DMRG, TDVP, Krylov-based MPS evolution, and MPO-based evolution are often effective at short and intermediate times \cite{vidalEfficientSimulationOneDimensional2004a,whiteRealTimeEvolutionUsing2004,daleyTimedependentDensitymatrixRenormalizationgroup2004,haegemanUnifyingTimeEvolution2016,zaletelTimeevolvingMatrixProduct2015}.
In higher dimensions, PEPS and infinite PEPS extend the idea but require approximate tensor-environment updates \cite{verstraeteCriticalityAreaLaw2006,jordanClassicalSimulationInfiniteSize2008,jiangAccurateDeterminationTensor2008a,pizornTimeEvolutionProjected2011,phienInfiniteProjectedEntangled2015,hubigTimedependentStudyDisordered2019}.
The central difficulty is that real-time evolution can generate entanglement rapidly \cite{calabreseEvolutionEntanglementEntropy2005}: an MPS bond dimension sufficient for the full \(\ket{\Psi(t)}\) may grow exponentially with time, and higher-dimensional updates add costly environment contractions.
This motivates the question studied here: if the target is a local observable, amplitude, or response function, can one avoid representing the full time-evolved state and compress only the information needed for the final contraction?

Recent tensor-network methods address this question in several ways.
Fixed-bond-dimension TDVP can reproduce hydrodynamic local thermalization even when the microscopic wavefunction is highly entangled \cite{leviatanQuantumThermalizationDynamics,hallamLyapunovSpectraQuantum2019}.
Adaptive fermionic mode transformations and mixed energy/spatial bases reduce apparent entanglement by choosing a more natural single-particle basis \cite{krumnowOvercomingEntanglementBarrier2019,pastoriDisentanglingSourcesQuantum2019,ramsBreakingEntanglementBarrier2020}.
Mixed-state and local-density-matrix approaches trade long-range entanglement for mixture or local information while preserving local observables \cite{whiteQuantumDynamicsThermalizing2018,suraceSimulatingOutofequilibriumDynamics2019,frias-perezConvertingLongRangeEntanglement2024a,kleinkvorningTimeevolutionLocalInformation2022}.
Heisenberg-picture methods such as dissipation-assisted operator evolution instead compress the slow local operator subspace relevant for hydrodynamic transport \cite{rakovszkyDissipationassistedOperatorEvolution2022a}.
All of these approaches exploit the fact that local dynamical information can be simpler than the full state.

\subsection{Spacetime tensor network contraction}
For the target observable, one can also work directly with the spacetime tensor network. 
Starting in \cite{banulsMatrixProductStates2009b}, the two-dimensional network for \(\langle \Psi(t)|O|\Psi(t)\rangle\) is contracted along the spatial direction after folding the ket and bra time evolutions, so the compressed object is a temporal boundary MPS rather than the evolved state itself. 
This idea was refined by Frías-Pérez and Bañuls \cite{frias-perezLightConeTensor2022}, who used the exact light-cone structure of the local observable to remove canceling gates outside the causal cone and contract only the minimal spacetime network. 
These spacetime-contraction methods avoid representing the full time-evolved state, but their cost is still controlled by the entanglement of the transverse temporal environment. 
This motivates the question central to this summary: can the temporal environment required for a target observable be compressed and propagated efficiently, even when the full wavefunction cannot be?

\section{Methods and Results}

\subsection{MPO+MPS compression methods}

When applying an MPO \(W\) to an MPS \(\ket{\psi}\), the exact product \(W\ket{\psi}\) is again an MPS, but its bond dimension is enlarged from \(\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\) to roughly \(D\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\), $D$ the bond dimension of the MPO.
The purpose of MPO-MPS compression is therefore to construct a lower-bond-dimension approximation \(\ket{\eta}\approx W\ket{\psi}\), with bond dimension at most \(\chi\), while retaining the information relevant to later contractions or observables. 
Different compression algorithms differ mainly in whether they first form the enlarged product explicitly, whether they optimize the output MPS variationally, and whether the truncation uses local or global information about the tensor-network environment.

Direct, or contract-then-compress, compression first forms the enlarged MPS \(W\ket{\psi}\), canonicalizes it, and truncates by SVD \cite{grayQuimbPythonPackage2018}.
It is robust and close to optimal for the formed product, but temporarily carries the enlarged bond dimension \(D\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\), giving a relatively expensive \(O(nD^3\chi_{\mathrm{in}}^3)\)-type scaling.
Zip-up compression avoids this enlarged intermediate by sweeping across the chain, absorbing MPO tensors into the MPS and truncating immediately \cite{stoudenmireMinimallyEntangledTypical2010a}.
This is fast, typically \(O(nD\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\chi^2)\) at fixed physical dimension, but the truncations use only partial environments and can be less accurate than more global methods.

Successive randomized compression (SRC) uses randomized low-rank projections to identify the important subspaces of \(W\ket{\psi}\) in one pass \cite{camanoSuccessiveRandomizedCompression2026b}.
It avoids both the enlarged exact product and iterative fitting, with a cost comparable to fast single-pass methods, but its accuracy depends on the sampled subspace and should be checked by oversampling or smaller benchmark calculations.
Variational fitting instead solves
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\ket{\eta}\in \mathrm{MPS}_{\chi}} \| \ket{\eta}-W\ket{\psi}\|^2 .
\end{equation}
It is flexible and can refine a good initial guess, but is iterative, nonconvex, and may converge slowly for difficult MPOs.
These methods expose the basic tradeoff: direct compression is reliable but expensive, zip-up is fast but locally informed, SRC is fast but randomized, and fitting is flexible but iterative.
The density-matrix and transition-matrix methods below instead choose truncation bases from reduced environments, which is the form most relevant for spacetime contraction.

 \begin{figure}
     \centering
     \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{RDM_Compression.png}
     \caption{Density-matrix compression of an MPS \(A\). Reduced density matrices of \(A\otimes A^\dagger\) select the dominant basis used to construct the compressed MPS \(A'\).}
     \label{fig:rdmCompression}
 \end{figure}

Density-matrix compression \cite{grayQuimbPythonPackage2018, TensorNetwork} selects the output basis from reduced density matrices of the target network.
For the MPS problem in Fig.~\ref{fig:rdmCompression}, one forms \(A\otimes A^\dagger\), traces out one side of each bipartition, diagonalizes the resulting reduced density matrix, and keeps the leading eigenvectors.
For MPO-MPS multiplication, the same construction is applied to \((W\ket{\psi})(W\ket{\psi})^\dagger\), giving a non-iterative, globally informed approximation close to direct contract-then-compress without relying on zip-up's local truncations or fitting's nonlinear sweeps.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{RTM_Compression.png}
    \caption{RTM compression of MPSs \(A\) and \(B\). The reduced transition matrix of \(A\otimes B\) is truncated by SVD, producing paired bases that preserve the \(A\)-\(B\) contraction.}
    \label{fig:rtmCompression}
\end{figure}

Reduced-transition-matrix (RTM) compression is closely analogous to density-matrix compression, but the object being preserved is different. 
Instead of optimizing a single MPS norm through \(A\otimes A^\dagger\), RTM starts from two MPSs \(A\) and \(B\) and compresses the mixed transition network \(A\otimes B\), so the target is their contraction rather than either state separately.
For a bipartition,
\begin{equation}
    \rho^{AB}_{i\cdots N}=\mathrm{Tr}_{1\cdots i-1}[A\otimes B]
\end{equation}
is generally non-Hermitian, so it is truncated by SVD rather than by an eigenvalue decomposition,
\begin{equation}
    \rho^{AB} \simeq U S V^\dagger 
\end{equation}
The left and right singular vectors define paired truncation bases for \(A\) and \(B\), as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rtmCompression}.
RTM compression \cite{Carignano2024TemporalEntropy, carignanoITransversejlLibraryTransverse2025, carignanoOvercomingEntanglementBarrier2025} is therefore naturally adapted to transverse tensor-network contraction, where the observable is an overlap between left and right temporal boundaries.
It also avoids the gauge mismatch of separately compressing non-Hermitian temporal MPSs: the retained directions are selected by their contribution to the mixed contraction.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{RTM_and_RDM_error.png}
    \caption{Overlap error for 1000 random MPS pairs compressed from bond dimension \(12\) to \(6\). RTM preserves \(\langle \psi_1|\psi_2\rangle\) much more accurately than separate RDM compression.}
    \label{fig:rtmRdmError}
\end{figure}

As a first check, Fig.~\ref{fig:rtmRdmError} compares RTM and separate RDM compression on random MPS pairs of length \(L=8\).
Both states are compressed from bond dimension \(12\) to \(6\), and the error is measured in the final overlap.
The RTM errors are concentrated near zero, while separate RDM compression produces a broader distribution, confirming that the transition matrix keeps directions relevant to the contraction rather than to the individual MPS norms.

\subsection{1D spacetime tensor network contraction}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{1D_TN_amplitude.png}
    \caption{Spatial contraction of a 1D spacetime tensor network for \(\langle \mathbf n|\psi(t)\rangle\). Each column is a temporal MPO \(E_i\), and left/right temporal MPS boundaries give the amplitude by overlap.}
    \label{fig:1dTnAmplitude}
\end{figure}

For one-dimensional dynamics, the amplitude \(\langle \mathbf n|\psi(t)\rangle=\langle \mathbf n|U(t)|\psi_0\rangle\) becomes a two-dimensional spacetime tensor network after Trotterizing \(U(t)\).
Instead of contracting in the time direction, one can contract spatially \cite{banulsMatrixProductStates2009b,hastingsConnectingEntanglementTime2015,carignanoOvercomingEntanglementBarrier2025}.
Each column is grouped into a temporal MPO \(E_i\), and the left/right temporal MPS boundaries obey
\begin{equation}
\langle L_{i+1}|=\langle L_i|E_{i+1},
\qquad
|R_i\rangle=E_i|R_{i+1}\rangle    
\end{equation}
With exact contractions, the amplitude is independent of the spatial cut,
\begin{equation}
    \langle \mathbf n|\psi(t)\rangle=\langle L_i|R_{i+1}\rangle
\end{equation}
for any \(i\). 
Compression is needed after each column, but the object being compressed is now a temporal boundary, not the full \(\ket{\psi(t)}\).
For amplitudes, the relevant error is therefore the final overlap \(\langle L_i|R_{i+1}\rangle\), making RTM compression naturally suited to the contraction.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{1D_TN_observable.png}
    \caption{Folded spacetime tensor network for \(O(t)=\langle \psi(t)|\hat O|\psi(t)\rangle\). The local operator modifies one temporal MPO \(E_O\), and the observable is \(\langle L|E_O|R\rangle\).}
    \label{fig:1dTnObservable}
\end{figure}

For a local observable, the expectation value is
\begin{equation}
O(t)=\langle \psi(t)|\hat O|\psi(t)\rangle=\langle \psi_0|U^\dagger(t)\hat O U(t)|\psi_0\rangle
\end{equation}
Trotterizing \(U(t)\) and \(U^\dagger(t)\) gives a double-layer spacetime network.
Folding pairs the forward and backward tensors at the same spatial location \cite{banulsMatrixProductStates2009b,hastingsConnectingEntanglementTime2015,Carignano2024TemporalEntropy}, producing environment temporal MPOs \(E_i\) and a modified observable column \(E_O\).
The local expectation value is then
\begin{equation}
O(t)=\langle L|E_O|R\rangle .
\end{equation}
Thus \(\langle L|\) and \(|R\rangle\) are influence-functional MPSs for the local subsystem containing the observable.

\subsubsection{Finite chains}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{1D_Finite_Amplitude.png}
    \caption{Finite-chain Loschmidt amplitude for the 1D TFIM with \(N=18\), \(h_z=0\), \(h_x=-1.5\), and initial state \(\ket{\mathbf 0}\). RTM tracks the TEBD \(\chi=512\) benchmark more accurately than RDM at low temporal bond dimension.}
    \label{fig:1dFiniteAmplitude}
\end{figure}

For finite chains, we first initialize left and right temporal MPSs using density-matrix compression,
\begin{equation}
\langle L_{i+1}^{(0)}|\simeq \mathcal C_{\mathrm{DM}}\!\left[\langle L_i^{(0)}|E_{i+1}\right],
\end{equation}
with the analogous right-boundary update
\begin{equation}
|R_i^{(0)}\rangle\simeq \mathcal C_{\mathrm{DM}}\!\left[E_i|R_{i+1}^{(0)}\rangle\right].
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal C_{\mathrm{DM}}\) truncates to the chosen bond dimension.
RTM then performs environment-dependent sweeps: a left-to-right update compresses \(\langle L_{i+1}|\) using \(|R_{i+1}\rangle\) as the environment, followed by a right-to-left update using the improved left boundaries.
One or two bidirectional sweeps are usually sufficient, and Fig.~\ref{fig:1dFiniteAmplitude} shows that this preserves the final overlap better than independently compressing each boundary.
For local observables, folding keeps \(U(t)\) and \(U^\dagger(t)\) cancellations inside each temporal tensor, while the modified \(E_O\) makes the contraction explicitly inhomogeneous.
RTM is useful here because the boundary is truncated with the opposite boundary and observable region in mind, rather than by its standalone norm.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{1D_Finite_Observable.png}
    \caption{Finite-chain middle-site magnetization \(Z_{N/2}(t)\) for the same TFIM as Fig.~\ref{fig:1dFiniteAmplitude}. Folded RTM gives the closest low-bond-dimension transverse-contraction result to the TEBD \(\chi=128\) benchmark.}
    \label{fig:1dFiniteObservable}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:1dFiniteObservable} shows that folding improves stability and folded RTM is closer to the TEBD benchmark than folded RDM or fitting-based compression, demonstrating the same contraction-aware advantage for local observables.

\subsubsection{Infinite chains}

For an infinite translationally invariant chain, the finite sweep is replaced by a power method for the dominant temporal boundary MPS of the spatial transfer matrix \cite{Carignano2024TemporalEntropy,carignanoITransversejlLibraryTransverse2025,carignanoOvercomingEntanglementBarrier2025}:
\begin{equation}
\langle L^{(n+1)}|\simeq \mathcal C\!\left[\langle L^{(n)}|E\right],
\end{equation}
The compression can be RDM or RTM, with the latter using the opposite fixed-point boundary as the environment.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{1D_Infinite_Amplitude.png}
    \caption{Infinite-chain Loschmidt free-energy rate for the 1D TFIM with \(h_z=0\), \(h_x=-1.5\), and initial state \(\ket{\mathbf 0}\). RTM follows the analytical result more closely than RDM.}
    \label{fig:1dInfiniteAmplitude}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:1dInfiniteAmplitude} shows that RTM gives a more stable dominant transverse eigenvalue, and hence a more accurate Loschmidt free-energy rate, than RDM.


\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{1D_Infinite_Entropy.png}
    \caption{Temporal entropy diagnostics for the infinite-chain amplitude. The transition-matrix entropy \(S_{LR}\) is smaller and closer to \(\log(T)\) than the single-boundary entropy \(S_{LL}\).}
    \label{fig:1dInfiniteEntropy}
\end{figure}

This agrees with the temporal-entropy theory of Refs.~\cite{Carignano2024TemporalEntropy,carignanoOvercomingEntanglementBarrier2025}: for translationally invariant amplitude networks, the relevant transition-matrix entropy \(S_{LR}\) saturates or grows at most logarithmically with time.
Figure~\ref{fig:1dInfiniteEntropy} shows that \(S_{LL}\), which measures one boundary in isolation, can grow much faster and overestimate the cost of the contraction.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{1D_Infinite_Observable.png}
    \caption{Infinite-chain local magnetization \(Z(t)\) for the 1D TFIM with \(h_z=0\), \(h_x=-1.5\), and initial state \(\ket{+}\). RTM tracks the TEBD benchmark longer than RDM.}
    \label{fig:1dInfiniteObservable}
\end{figure}

For local observables, \(E_O\) creates an inhomogeneous insertion, but the fixed-point environments still contract the observable as \(\langle L|E_O|R\rangle\).
Figures~\ref{fig:1dInfiniteObservable} and \ref{fig:1dInfiniteObservableSupp} show that RTM remains advantageous beyond amplitudes, although long-time fixed-point convergence can occasionally fail.

\subsection{Tree spacetime tensor network contraction}

The 1D transverse contraction is a special case of message passing: the left and right temporal MPSs are messages sent toward the target contraction.
On a tree lattice this becomes literal, with each branch summarized by a directed temporal MPS message.
Belief propagation is exact on trees and approximate only in the presence of loops \cite{yedidiaUnderstandingBeliefPropagation2003,alkabetzTensorNetworksContraction2021}; in the influence-functional setting, the messages are temporal MPSs rather than scalar probabilities \cite{parkSimulatingQuantumDynamics2025}.
Thus, for a fixed Trotterized tree network, the main approximation is message compression, making trees a clean setting for comparing RDM and RTM.

\subsubsection{Finite trees}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Message_Propagation.png}
    \caption{Finite-tree message update. RDM compresses \(m_{3\to4}\) from \(T_3\) and incoming messages, while RTM also uses the reverse message \(m_{4\to3}\) as the contraction environment.}
    \label{fig:messagePropagation}
\end{figure}

Each directed edge \(i\to j\) carries a temporal MPS message \(m_{i\to j}\).
The RDM update contracts the local temporal tensor \(T_i\) with all incoming messages except from the target direction,
\begin{equation}
m_{i\to j}\simeq
\mathcal C_{\mathrm{RDM}}\!\left[
T_i\prod_{k\in \partial i\setminus j}m_{k\to i}
\right],
\end{equation}
where \(\partial i\) denotes the neighbors of \(i\).
RTM uses the same candidate message but compresses it with the reverse message as environment,
\begin{equation}
m_{i\to j}\simeq
\mathcal C_{\mathrm{RTM}}\!\left[
T_i\prod_{k\in \partial i\setminus j}m_{k\to i};
m_{j\to i}
\right],
\end{equation}
so the truncation preserves the mixed contraction across edge \((i,j)\).
In practice, all directed messages are first initialized by RDM leaf-to-root and root-to-leaf passes.
RTM then alternates leaf-to-root and root-to-leaf sweeps using the current reverse messages as environments until the compressed BP equations converge.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Tree_Finite_Amplitude.png}
    \caption{Finite-tree Loschmidt amplitude on a depth-4 binary TFIM tree with \(h_z=0.6\), \(h_x=-1.0\), and initial state \(\ket{\mathbf 0}\). RTM gives smaller amplitude error than DM, especially at \(t=6.5\).}
    \label{fig:treeFiniteAmplitude}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:treeFiniteAmplitude} tests this finite-tree sweep on a binary-tree Loschmidt amplitude.
Both methods improve with \(\chi\), but RTM gives smaller error, showing that contraction-aware compression remains useful beyond chains.
The gain is more modest than in 1D because the tree has shorter paths and many branching messages, but the same principle holds.


\subsubsection{Infinite trees}

For an infinite Cayley tree with coordination \(z=3\), rotational symmetry reduces message propagation to a unit-cell fixed point.
One repeatedly updates a representative temporal MPS message using the \(z-1\) incoming branches, with either independent RDM compression or TM compression using the reverse fixed-point message as environment.
This is the tree analogue of the infinite-chain power method.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Tree_Infinite_Observable.png}
    \caption{Local magnetization \(X(t)\) on an infinite \(z=3\) Cayley tree with \(h_x=-2.0\) and initial state \(\ket{+}\). TM stays closer to the simple-update \(\chi=64\) benchmark than DM.}
    \label{fig:treeInfiniteObservable}
\end{figure}

Figures~\ref{fig:treeInfiniteObservable} and \ref{fig:treeInfiniteObservableError} show the infinite-tree result.
RDM typically drifts from the simple-update benchmark earlier, while converged TM remains closer across the shown fields and bond dimensions.
Some long-time TM fixed points fail to converge cleanly, indicating that environment-dependent compression improves the contraction when stable but can make the nonlinear iteration harder.

\subsection{2D spacetime tensor network contraction}

We next test an infinite square lattice with a one-site unit cell.
Finite 2D MPS-BP updates are not yet robust, while the infinite lattice reduces to a unit-cell fixed point.
Unlike the tree case, however, square-lattice BP is only an approximation: single-site BP effectively replaces the loopy square lattice by a \(z=4\) Bethe lattice, so the observable contains both message-compression error and loop error.
Systematic 2D accuracy will require cluster expansions or related loop corrections \cite{parkSimulatingQuantumDynamics2025}, but the unit-cell setting still allows a direct comparison of DM and TM message compression.

    \label{fig:2dInfiniteObservable}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:2dInfiniteObservable} shows that TM stays close to the fit-compressed IF-BP reference longer than DM.
The remaining discrepancy is not only compression error, because the single-site BP approximation also misses square-lattice loops.

\section{Discussion}

The central message of this work is that temporal messages should be compressed with awareness of the contraction in which they will be used.
RDM compression approximates each temporal MPS message as an isolated object, while amplitudes, observables, and BP contractions depend on overlaps or local contractions between several messages.
RTM compression instead selects the truncated subspace using the opposite boundary or reverse message as an environment.
Across the one-dimensional, tree, and two-dimensional tests above, this environment-dependent choice usually delays the onset of systematic deviation at fixed bond dimension.

For local observables, the present implementation used full environment messages rather than rebuilding a separate light cone for each operator.
Figure~\ref{fig:1dFiniteObservableCompare} gives the numerical motivation: in the one-dimensional chain, the influence messages on the two sides of the measured site are nearly independent of the operator inserted at the center.
This supports the use of reusable operator-independent messages in the tree and two-dimensional calculations, but it should still be checked against explicit light-cone contractions, especially when operator spreading or conserved modes become important.

In two dimensions, message compression is only one source of error.
Single-site BP on the square lattice replaces the loopy lattice by a Bethe-lattice approximation, so loop correlations are missing even if every temporal message is accurately compressed.
Quantitatively controlled two-dimensional dynamics will therefore require cluster expansions or related loop-correction schemes \cite{parkSimulatingQuantumDynamics2025}.
The value of RTM in that setting is cost reduction: if TM-compressed messages reproduce the relevant cluster contributions at smaller \(\chi\), cluster-expanded IF-BP becomes cheaper.
Figure~\ref{fig:treeZ4Observable} isolates this issue on the corresponding \(z=4\) infinite tree, where BP is exact and the remaining discrepancy mainly comes from message compression.

Several extensions are natural.
First, the message ansatz can be enlarged.
This work represents each directed message as a temporal MPS, but loopy or higher-dimensional geometries may benefit from block messages or PEPS-like messages that retain short-loop and multi-branch correlations explicitly \cite{guoBlockBeliefPropagation2023,wangTensorNetworkMessage2023}.
Second, the compression itself could be made variational.
RTM is a non-variational truncation based on the reduced transition matrix; an optimized fitting procedure targeting the final local contraction or BP residual may give a better environment-dependent message at the same bond dimension.

The main algorithmic issue is convergence.
TM compression often gives better converged messages, but the nonlinear fixed-point iteration can be less stable than RDM at long times or high connectivity.
Convergence accelerators such as DIIS \cite{pulayConvergenceAccelerationIterative1980,pulayImprovedSCFConvergence1982} may be useful for infinite-unit-cell updates and finite-size environment-dependent BP sweeps.
More systematic diagnostics are also needed, including deviation time versus bond dimension, error as a function of \(T\) and \(\chi\), and temporal entanglement or transition-matrix entropy for chains, trees, and loopy BP approximations.

Overall, the results support treating quantum-dynamical BP messages as contraction-dependent objects rather than standalone states.
Environment-dependent compression aligns the numerical representation with the target observable, and provides a practical route toward longer-time simulations of local quantum dynamics.
