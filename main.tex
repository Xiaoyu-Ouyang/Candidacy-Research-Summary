\section{Background}
\subsection{Overview of equilibrium and non-equilibrium many-body physics}
Since the formulation of quantum mechanics, understanding the quantum many-body physics, namely the emergent collective behavior arising from many interacting quantum particles, has become one of the central problems in physics, chemistry and material science. 
The microscopic principle behind the quantum many-body system is simply the Schrodinger equation $H \ket{\Psi(t)}=i \hbar \partial_t \ket{\Psi(t)}$ with the physical Hilbert space restricted by particle-exchange symmetry. 
But rich collective behaviors, such as superconductivity \cite{bardeenTheorySuperconductivity1957}, quantum phase transition \cite{sachdevQuantumPhaseTransitions2011a}, topological order \cite{wenTopologicalOrdersRigid1990} under equilibrium, or thermalization \cite{srednickiChaosQuantumThermalization1994a}, many-body localization \cite{nandkishoreManyBodyLocalizationThermalization2015a} under non-equilibrium, could emerge from the many-body Hamiltonian, making the many-body physics interesting and mysterious. 
Beyond the intrinsic interest, practical applications, such as predicting molecular energies \cite{kohnSelfConsistentEquationsIncluding1965}, materials properties \cite{hohenbergInhomogeneousElectronGas1964} under equilibrium, and describing spectra \cite{rungeDensityFunctionalTheoryTimeDependent1984}, transport properties \cite{meirLandauerFormulaCurrent1992}, relaxation dynamics under non-equilibrium, could hugely benefit from accurately solving quantum many-body problems.

However the exponential growth of the many-body Hilbert space leads to computational difficulties. 
For a system of $N$ local degrees of freedom with local dimension $d$, a generic state requires $d^N$ complex amplitudes to represent exactly, hence exact classical representation rapidly become infeasible as $N$ increases.

Different physical questions require different levels of information about the many-body state.  
One central task to solve is the ground state problem $H\ket{\Psi}=E\ket{\Psi}$ of the quantum many-body system, where the primary goal is often to
obtain the ground-state energy, selected expectation values, and, when needed, a compact representation of the ground-state wavefunction itself.
A broad range of classical methods have been developed for this purpose, including mean-field approaches such as Hartree-Fock \cite{roothaanNewDevelopmentsMolecular1951}, systematically
improvable wavefunction methods such as coupled cluster \cite{bartlettCoupledclusterTheoryQuantum2007}, and
variational tensor-network methods such as density-matrix
renormalization group (DMRG) \cite{whiteDensityMatrixFormulation1992b}.
These methods succeed by exploiting
additional structure of physically relevant states, rather than by
representing an arbitrary vector in the full Hilbert space.

Tensor networks provide one particularly important example of this
principle. 
In many low-energy systems with local interactions, ground
states obey an area law for entanglement entropy \cite{eisertColloquiumAreaLaws2010}, allowing them to be efficiently approximated by matrix product states or more general tensor-network ansatzes. 
This observation leads to the success of DMRG in one-dimensional systems and motivates tensor-network approaches to larger classes of many-body problems.

However, the situation is more difficult for nonequilibrium real-time dynamics. In this setting, the goal is often to predict time-dependent observables, response functions, spectra, transport properties, or relaxation processes rather than only equilibrium energies. Different numerical approaches can be viewed as choosing different dynamical objects to propagate: a state vector, a correlation function, a density, a reduced density matrix, or an operator. Exact diagonalization, Krylov methods, and Chebyshev expansions \cite{AnalysisKrylovSubspace, hochbruckKrylovSubspaceApproximations1997, tal-ezerAccurateEfficientScheme1984} compute the action of the time-evolution operator on a state without explicitly forming \(e^{-iHt}\), but they still operate in the full Hilbert space and are therefore limited to relatively small systems.

Other methods avoid propagating the full wavefunction by targeting reduced objects directly. Linear-response and Green's-function approaches \cite{fetterQuantumTheoryManyparticle2003} formulate spectra and transport in terms of correlation functions rather than the full trajectory \(\ket{\Psi(t)}\), but their practical accuracy depends on approximations to response kernels, self-energies, and vertex corrections. Time-dependent density-functional theory (TDDFT) \cite{rungeDensityFunctionalTheoryTimeDependent1984} replaces the many-body wavefunction by the time-dependent density, greatly reducing the complexity but introducing exchange-correlation functionals whose accuracy is highly system and regime dependent. 

Sparse Pauli dynamics and related Pauli-propagation methods \cite{rallSimulationQubitQuantum2019, begusicFastConvergedClassical2024a, begusicRealTimeOperatorEvolution2025, rudolphPauliPropagationComputational2025} take another Heisenberg-picture route: the observable is expanded as \(O(t)=\sum_P a_P(t)P\) in the Pauli-string basis, and the dynamics is approximated by retaining only the important Pauli strings. This avoids state evolution when the relevant operator remains sparse or approximately compressible in the Pauli basis, but the cost can still grow rapidly through Pauli-string proliferation and possible backflow from discarded strings.

A related but conceptually distinct reduction is to separate the degrees of freedom into a subsystem of interest and an environment \cite{breuerTheoryOpenQuantum2002, nakajimaQuantumTheoryTransport1958a, zwanzigEnsembleMethodTheory1960}. In this reduced-dynamics viewpoint, the target object is not the full state \(\rho_{SE}(t)\), but the reduced density matrix \(\rho_S(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_E \rho_{SE}(t)\) or observables supported on \(S\). Markovian master equations and Lindblad dynamics \cite{goriniCompletelyPositiveDynamical1976, lindbladGeneratorsQuantumDynamical1976a} approximate the environmental influence as memoryless dissipation, leading to closed equations for \(\rho_S(t)\). More general approaches, such as hierarchical equations of motion (HEOM) \cite{tanimuraTimeEvolutionQuantum1989, tanimuraNumericallyExactApproach2020} and influence-functional path integrals \cite{feynmanTheoryGeneralQuantum1963, makriNumericalPathIntegral1995}, retain non-Markovian memory through auxiliary density matrices or temporal kernels. These methods can be highly accurate when the system-environment split and bath model are appropriate, but their cost often grows rapidly with bath memory time, coupling strength, or the number of subsystem degrees of freedom. Thus, reduced-dynamics methods trade the exponential complexity of the full wavefunction for the problem of accurately and compactly representing the environment's influence.

\subsection{Current tensor network methods for quantum dynamics}
Tensor network time-evolution methods provide another route by compressing the state, operator, or path-integral object leveraging entanglement or correlation structure of the system \cite{orusPracticalIntroductionTensor2014a, paeckelTimeevolutionMethodsMatrixproduct2019}. 
In one-dimensional systems, methods such as TEBD, time-dependent DMRG, TDVP, Krylov-based MPS evolution, and MPO-based time evolution \cite{vidalEfficientSimulationOneDimensional2004a, whiteRealTimeEvolutionUsing2004, daleyTimedependentDensitymatrixRenormalizationgroup2004, haegemanUnifyingTimeEvolution2016, zaletelTimeevolvingMatrixProduct2015} can be very effective at short and intermediate times.
In higher-dimensional systems, projected entangled-pair states and infinite PEPS extend the tensor-network approach beyond one dimension \cite{verstraeteCriticalityAreaLaw2006, jordanClassicalSimulationInfiniteSize2008, jiangAccurateDeterminationTensor2008a, pizornTimeEvolutionProjected2011, phienInfiniteProjectedEntangled2015, hubigTimedependentStudyDisordered2019}, with real-time evolution implemented through local gates and approximate tensor updates such as simple update, full update, or related environment-based schemes.

The main limitation is that real-time evolution can generate entanglement rapidly \cite{calabreseEvolutionEntanglementEntropy2005}. 
After a quench or under generic continuous real-time evolution, the entanglement entropy of \(\ket{\Psi(t)}\) often grows with time.
In one-dimensional MPS methods, this growth translates into an increasing bond dimension, which may grow exponentially with time for an accurate representation of the full state.
In higher dimensions, the difficulty is compounded by the cost of approximately contracting the tensor-network environment during the update.
As a result, methods that are highly effective for ground states can become inefficient or uncontrolled for long-time dynamics.
This motivates a more targeted question: when the desired output is not the full wavefunction but a local observable, amplitude, or response function, can one avoid representing the entire time-evolved state and instead compress only the information relevant to the final contraction?

We can now give a brief summary of several recent notable tensor network methods trying to deal with the time evolution problem in better approaches, especially by focusing on the local description of the state to circumvent the exponential complexity that originated from nonlocal correlations. 

Several recent tensor-network approaches can be understood as changing the object that is compressed, rather than trying to accurately represent the full time-evolved wavefunction. One direction keeps a state-based MPS description but interprets it as an effective description of local thermalizing dynamics, rather than as an accurate microscopic wavefunction. Leviatan et al. \cite{leviatanQuantumThermalizationDynamics} showed that fixed-bond-dimension TDVP can accurately capture local thermalization, diffusion, and transport in thermalizing closed systems, even after the exact wavefunction has developed volume-law entanglement, because long-time local observables are governed by emergent hydrodynamics rather than detailed nonlocal entanglement. Hallam et al. \cite{hallamLyapunovSpectraQuantum2019} further analyzed this TDVP-projected dynamics through its Lyapunov spectrum, interpreting the projected tensor-network dynamics as an effective classical chaotic system. This approach is most natural for thermalizing systems and local hydrodynamic observables, and is less controlled when coherent nonlocal information remains important.

A related state-based strategy is to change the basis in which the MPS is written. Krumnow et al. \cite{krumnowOvercomingEntanglementBarrier2019} enlarged the MPS ansatz by dynamically rotating fermionic modes, arguing that part of the apparent entanglement barrier comes from using a fixed real-space basis. Pastori et al. \cite{pastoriDisentanglingSourcesQuantum2019} supported this viewpoint by showing that quench-generated entanglement can contain a large basis-dependent single-particle component. Rams and Zwolak \cite{ramsBreakingEntanglementBarrier2020} applied a related idea to quantum transport, where a mixed energy/spatial basis localizes the relevant correlations near the bias window and allows current dynamics to be simulated to much longer times. These methods suggest that some entanglement growth reflects a poor choice of basis rather than irreducible many-body complexity, although the optimal basis is problem dependent and the transformed Hamiltonian or MPO can become more complicated.

Another class replaces pure-state evolution by mixed-state or local-density-matrix evolution. In density matrix truncation, White et al. \cite{whiteQuantumDynamicsThermalizing2018} evolved a matrix product density operator and truncated it while preserving the trace and local operator expectation values, so conserved densities and hydrodynamic behavior are not destroyed by compression. 
Surace et al. \cite{suraceSimulatingOutofequilibriumDynamics2019} formulated a related local-indistinguishability principle, showing that in free-fermion quenches an entangled pure state can be replaced by a mixed state with the same short-range reduced density matrices while retaining robust equilibration values of local observables. 
Frías-Pérez et al. \cite{frias-perezConvertingLongRangeEntanglement2024a} developed this idea into an explicit tensor-network algorithm that identifies long-range entanglement carried by fast degrees of freedom and converts it into mixture, producing a bounded-bond MPO description for local equilibration. 
Klein Kvorning et al. \cite{kleinkvorningTimeevolutionLocalInformation2022} further developed a related local-information viewpoint using an information lattice, evolving finite sets of local density matrices while modeling the flow of information to larger length scales. These approaches trade long-range entanglement for mixture or local information, but their accuracy depends on whether discarded long-range correlations or information currents feed back into later local observables.

One can also avoid state evolution entirely by working in the Heisenberg picture. In dissipation-assisted operator evolution, Rakovszky et al. \cite{rakovszkyDissipationassistedOperatorEvolution2022a} evolved local conserved-density operators as MPOs and added artificial dissipation that suppresses long Pauli strings responsible for operator entanglement growth. Transport coefficients are then estimated by extrapolating the artificial dissipation to zero. This method is well matched to high-temperature hydrodynamic transport, but relies on weak backflow from discarded long operators into the slow local conserved-density subspace.

\subsection{Spacetime tensor network contraction}
For the target observable, one can also work directly with the spacetime tensor network. 
Starting in \cite{banulsMatrixProductStates2009b}, the two-dimensional network for \(\langle \Psi(t)|O|\Psi(t)\rangle\) is contracted along the spatial direction after folding the ket and bra time evolutions, so the compressed object is a temporal boundary MPS rather than the evolved state itself. 
This idea was refined by Frías-Pérez and Bañuls \cite{frias-perezLightConeTensor2022}, who used the exact light-cone structure of the local observable to remove canceling gates outside the causal cone and contract only the minimal spacetime network. 
These spacetime-contraction methods avoid representing the full time-evolved state, but their cost is still controlled by the entanglement of the transverse temporal environment. 
This motivates the question central to this summary: can the temporal environment required for a target observable be compressed and propagated efficiently, even when the full wavefunction cannot be?

\section{Methods and Results}

\subsection{MPO+MPS compression methods}

When applying an MPO \(W\) to an MPS \(\ket{\psi}\), the exact product \(W\ket{\psi}\) is again an MPS, but its bond dimension is enlarged from \(\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\) to roughly \(D\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\), $D$ the bond dimension of the MPO.
The purpose of MPO-MPS compression is therefore to construct a lower-bond-dimension approximation \(\ket{\eta}\approx W\ket{\psi}\), with bond dimension at most \(\chi\), while retaining the information relevant to later contractions or observables. 
Different compression algorithms differ mainly in whether they first form the enlarged product explicitly, whether they optimize the output MPS variationally, and whether the truncation uses local or global information about the tensor-network environment.

The most straightforward approach is the direct, or contract-then-compress, method \cite{grayQuimbPythonPackage2018}.
It first contracts the MPO and MPS site by site to form the exact enlarged MPS representation of \(W\ket{\psi}\), then brings this enlarged MPS into canonical form and performs standard SVD truncations to reduce the bond dimension.
This method is simple, robust, and works directly with singular values, which can make it numerically precise.
Its main drawback is cost: because it temporarily constructs the enlarged bond dimension \(D\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\), the scaling is relatively expensive, roughly \(O(nD^3\chi_{\mathrm{in}}^3)\) for fixed physical dimension in the standard estimate.
It is therefore useful as a reliable baseline or for small bond dimensions, but becomes inefficient when the MPO bond dimension or input MPS bond dimension is large.

The zip-up method was introduced as a faster alternative for MPO-MPS multiplication \cite{stoudenmireMinimallyEntangledTypical2010a}.
Instead of forming the full enlarged product first, it sweeps from one end of the chain to the other, locally absorbing the MPO tensors into the MPS tensors and immediately truncating the growing bond dimension.
In this sense, the target is still \(\ket{\eta}\approx W\ket{\psi}\), but the approximation is constructed progressively, before the exact product is ever fully formed.
Its cost is much lower than direct compression, with a typical fixed-\(d\) scaling comparable to \(O(nD\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\chi^2)\), making it attractive for repeated MPO applications in time evolution.
The weakness is that each truncation is made using only a partial or pseudo-canonical environment, rather than the full global environment of the final product.
As a result, zip-up is fast and often sufficiently accurate, but can be noticeably less accurate than more globally informed methods; it is also most natural when a fixed output bond dimension is specified.

Successive randomized compression (SRC) is a more recent single-pass method for the same compressed MPO-MPS product \cite{camanoSuccessiveRandomizedCompression2026b}.
The idea is to avoid both the enlarged exact product and iterative variational fitting by using randomized low-rank projections to identify the important subspace of the target \(W\ket{\psi}\) successively along the chain.
Operationally, SRC constructs the output MPS through a sequence of randomized QB decompositions of effective tensor-network unfoldings.
If \(W\ket{\psi}\) is exactly representable with bond dimension \(\chi\), SRC can recover it with probability one; otherwise, it gives a randomized compressed approximation.
Its asymptotic cost is comparable to the fastest methods, approximately \(O(ndD\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\chi(\chi_{\mathrm{in}}+\chi+dD))\), or \(O(nD\chi^3)\) in the common simplified regime \(d=O(1)\), \(D\leq \chi\), and \(\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\sim \chi\).
The advantage is that SRC is one-shot, fast, and often much more accurate than zip-up at similar cost.
The main limitation is that it is randomized and typically controlled by the chosen output bond dimension, so accuracy should be checked by oversampling or comparison with more accurate compression on smaller cases.

The variational fitting method formulates MPO-MPS compression as an optimization problem \cite{grayQuimbPythonPackage2018}:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\ket{\eta}\in \mathrm{MPS}_{\chi}} \| \ket{\eta}-W\ket{\psi}\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Instead of constructing \(W\ket{\psi}\) explicitly, it optimizes the tensors of \(\ket{\eta}\) by sweeping through the chain, in close analogy with one-site or two-site DMRG.
The target is therefore globally defined, and in principle the method can find a high-quality compressed MPS at relatively low per-sweep cost, again comparable to \(O(nD\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\chi^2)\) up to physical-dimension and local-contraction factors.
The advantage is flexibility: fitting can use a good initial guess, can optimize sums of tensor networks, and can refine a fast approximate result such as zip-up.
The drawback is that it is iterative and nonconvex. 
It may require many sweeps, can converge slowly, and can fail to converge for difficult MPOs, especially long-range or poorly conditioned operators.
 For this reason, fitting is often best used as a refinement step rather than the only compression method.

 These methods show a basic tradeoff in MPO-MPS compression: direct compression is reliable but expensive, zip-up is fast but locally informed, SRC is fast and globally randomized, and fitting is variationally flexible but iterative.
 The density-matrix and transfer-matrix based methods discussed next provide a different route, in which the truncation subspace is selected from reduced environments rather than from direct SVDs, randomized projections, or variational sweeps, and we will focus on the use of them during the work of spacetime tensor network contraction.

 \begin{figure}

     \caption{RDM compression of MPS $A$ by eigenvalue decomposition}
     \label{fig:rdmCompression}
 \end{figure}

The density-matrix compression method \cite{grayQuimbPythonPackage2018, TensorNetwork} selects the truncation subspace from reduced density matrices of the target tensor network.
In the simplest MPS compression problem shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rdmCompression}, the target is to approximate a high-bond-dimension MPS \(A\) by a lower-bond-dimension MPS \(A'\). Instead of locally truncating each bond by an SVD of a single tensor, one first forms the squared network \(A\otimes A^\dagger\), then partially traces out degrees of freedom to obtain reduced density matrices for successive bipartitions. 
Diagonalizing these reduced density matrices gives the dominant basis vectors \(U_i\), and keeping only the eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues defines the compressed tensors of \(A'\).

Operationally, the algorithm proceeds from one boundary of the chain.
One first contracts environments \(E_i\) of the squared network \(A\otimes A^\dagger\), then constructs the reduced density matrix for the last exposed site, for example \(\rho_6=\mathrm{Tr}_{1\cdots5}[A\otimes A^\dagger]\).
After diagonalizing \(\rho_6\), the leading eigenvectors define \(U_6\).
The algorithm then moves leftward: the already selected \(U_6\) is used to rotate and truncate the basis of the next reduced density matrix, \(\rho'_{56}\), whose leading eigenvectors define \(U_5\).
Repeating this procedure produces \(U_4,U_3,\ldots\), and finally the first tensor \(A'_1\), yielding a compressed MPS \(A'\) in a canonical gauge.
The key point is that each local tensor is chosen from the dominant eigenspace of a reduced environment, so the truncation is informed by the norm of the full target network, not only by a single local tensor.

For MPO-MPS multiplication, the same idea is applied to the product \(W\ket{\psi}\).
Rather than explicitly forming the enlarged MPS with bond dimension \(D\chi_{\mathrm{in}}\) and then compressing it, the density-matrix algorithm builds reduced density matrices of the squared MPO-MPS product \((W\ket{\psi})(W\ket{\psi})^\dagger\).
The leading eigenvectors of these reduced density matrices define the compressed output MPS \(\ket{\eta}\approx W\ket{\psi}\) with bond dimension \(\chi\).

Density-matrix compression is theoretically close to direct contract-then-compress: both select the dominant Schmidt subspace of the target \(W\ket{\psi}\) for a given output bond dimension.
The difference is procedural. 
Direct compression first forms the enlarged product MPS and then truncates it by canonical SVDs, whereas the density-matrix method obtains the same truncation subspaces from reduced density matrices of the doubled network \((W\ket{\psi})(W\ket{\psi})^\dagger\).
This makes the method accurate and non-iterative, and more globally informed than zip-up or local greedy truncation, while avoiding the convergence issues of variational fitting.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{RTM_Compression.png}
    \caption{RTM compression of MPS $A, B$ by SVD}
    \label{fig:rtmCompression}
\end{figure}

Reduced-transition-matrix (RTM) compression is closely analogous to density-matrix compression, but the object being preserved is different. 
In density-matrix compression, one constructs \(A\otimes A^\dagger\) and selects the dominant subspace of a single MPS \(A\), so the truncation is optimized for approximating \(A\) in norm.
In RTM compression, one instead starts from two MPSs, denoted here by \(A\) and \(B\), and constructs the mixed transition network \(A\otimes B\). 
The target is not to approximate either \(A\) or \(B\) individually, but to preserve their contraction, or more generally the contraction of a left and right temporal boundary appearing in a spacetime tensor network.

For a bipartition of the temporal chain, the reduced transition matrix is obtained by tracing out the complementary temporal degrees of freedom from the mixed object \(A\otimes B\).
Schematically, one may write
\begin{equation}
    \rho^{AB}_{i\cdots N}=\mathrm{Tr}_{1\cdots i-1}[A\otimes B]
\end{equation}
up to normalization by the full overlap.

Unlike a usual reduced density matrix, \(\rho^{AB}\) is generally non-Hermitian and need not have positive eigenvalues.
Therefore, the robust truncation is not an eigenvalue decomposition, but an SVD,
\begin{equation}
    \rho^{AB} \simeq U S V^\dagger 
\end{equation}

The leading left and right singular vectors define two different truncation bases: \(U\) is absorbed into the tensors of \(A\), while \(V^\dagger\) is absorbed into the tensors of \(B\). 
Repeating this procedure along the temporal chain produces compressed MPSs \(A'\) and \(B'\) whose bond dimension is reduced, but whose mutual contraction is preserved as accurately as possible.

The algorithm shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rtmCompression} follows the same sweeping structure as the density-matrix algorithm. 
One first builds environments of the mixed network \(A\otimes B\).
At the right boundary, the reduced transition matrix \(\rho_6=\mathrm{Tr}_{1\cdots5}[A\otimes B]\) is decomposed by SVD, giving \(U_6\), \(S_6\), and \(V_6^\dagger\).
The left singular vectors \(U_6\) define the compressed basis for the \(A\) boundary, while the right singular vectors \(V_6^\dagger\) define the corresponding compressed basis for the \(B\) boundary.
The procedure then moves leftward, constructing \(\rho'_{56}\), \(\rho'_{456}\), and so on, each time using the previously selected bases and keeping only the dominant singular directions.
At the end of the sweep, the compressed pair \(A'\) and \(B'\) is obtained.

RTM compression \cite{Carignano2024TemporalEntropy, carignanoITransversejlLibraryTransverse2025, carignanoOvercomingEntanglementBarrier2025} is directly adapted to the quantity computed in transverse tensor-network contraction. 
Standard RDM compression can be gauge-dependent for non-Hermitian transfer matrices: a gauge transformation can make the individual temporal MPSs appear easier or harder to compress without changing the final network contraction.
RTM compression avoids this mismatch by selecting the subspace from the mixed left-right transition matrix itself. 
Thus the retained singular vectors are chosen according to their contribution to the final overlap, rather than according to the individual norms of \(A\) and \(B\).

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{RTM_and_RDM_error.png}
    \caption{Overlap error distribution for RTM and RDM compression on 1000 random MPS pairs. Each pair has length \(L=8\) and initial bond dimension \(12\), and is compressed to bond dimension \(6\). The error is measured as \(\left|\langle \psi_1'|\psi_2'\rangle-\langle \psi_1|\psi_2\rangle\right|\). RTM compression preserves the overlap much more accurately than separately compressing the two MPSs by RDM compression.}
    \label{fig:rtmRdmError}
\end{figure}

To test whether RTM compression preserves the contraction-relevant information better than separate RDM compression, we first benchmarked the two methods on random MPS pairs.
For each sample, two random high-bond-dimension MPSs \(\ket{\psi_1}\) and \(\ket{\psi_2}\) of length \(L=8\) were generated with initial bond dimension \(12\).
Both methods were then used to compress the pair to bond dimension \(6\).
For RDM compression, each MPS was compressed separately according to its own reduced density matrices.
For RTM compression, the two MPSs were compressed together using the reduced transition matrix constructed from the mixed network \(\ket{\psi_2}\bra{\psi_1}\), so that the truncation basis was selected to preserve the overlap between the two states.

The resulting overlap errors are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rtmRdmError}. 
The RTM-compressed pairs are strongly concentrated near zero error, while the RDM-compressed pairs show a much broader distribution with a long tail.
RTM compression instead keeps the singular directions that contribute directly to the mixed contraction, leading to substantially smaller overlap error at the same output bond dimension.

\subsection{1D spacetime tensor network contraction}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{1D_TN_amplitude.png}
    \caption{Spatial contraction of a one-dimensional spacetime tensor network for the amplitude \(\langle \mathbf n|\psi(t)\rangle\). After Trotterizing the time evolution, each spatial column is interpreted as a temporal MPO \(E_i\). Contracting columns from the left and right produces temporal MPS boundaries \(\langle L_i|\) and \(|R_i\rangle\), and the full amplitude can be evaluated as their overlap across any spatial cut.}
    \label{fig:1dTnAmplitude}
\end{figure}

For one-dimensional real-time dynamics, the amplitude \(\langle \mathbf n|\psi(t)\rangle\) can be written as a two-dimensional spacetime tensor network. 
Starting from an initial state \(\ket{\psi_0}\), the time-evolved state is \(\ket{\psi(t)}=U(t)\ket{\psi_0}\). 
After a Trotter decomposition of \(U(t)\), the tensor network has one direction corresponding to space and the other corresponding to time. 
Fixing the final computational-basis configuration \(\mathbf n\) at the top boundary turns the network into a scalar contraction for the amplitude \(\langle \mathbf n|U(t)|\psi_0\rangle\).

Instead of contracting this network in the time direction, which corresponds to ordinary Schrödinger-picture MPS time evolution, one can contract it in the spatial direction \cite{banulsMatrixProductStates2009b,hastingsConnectingEntanglementTime2015,carignanoOvercomingEntanglementBarrier2025}.
In this transverse picture, each spatial column of the spacetime network is grouped into a temporal MPO \(E_i\), whose physical indices live along the time direction.
The partial contraction of all columns to the left of site \(i\) defines a left temporal MPS \(\langle L_i|\), while the partial contraction of all columns to the right defines a right temporal MPS \(|R_i\rangle\).
Applying one more column updates these temporal boundaries as
\begin{equation}
\langle L_{i+1}|=\langle L_i|E_{i+1},
\qquad
|R_i\rangle=E_i|R_{i+1}\rangle    
\end{equation}
With exact contractions, the amplitude is independent of the spatial cut and can be evaluated as
\begin{equation}
    \langle \mathbf n|\psi(t)\rangle=\langle L_i|R_{i+1}\rangle
\end{equation}
for any \(i\). 
In practice, however, each application of a temporal MPO increases the bond dimension of the temporal MPS, so compression is required after each spatial step.

This formulation changes the object being compressed. 
Standard MPS time evolution attempts to represent the full state \(\ket{\psi(t)}\), whose spatial entanglement can grow rapidly after a quench.
Transverse contraction instead represents the temporal boundary states \(\langle L_i|\) and \(|R_i\rangle\), whose bond dimension is controlled by temporal correlations in the spacetime network.
For amplitude calculations, the relevant accuracy criterion is therefore not the norm error of each temporal MPS separately, but the error in their final overlap \(\langle L_i|R_{i+1}\rangle\). 
This is the reason RTM compression, which selects the truncation basis from the mixed left-right transition matrix, is naturally suited to this contraction problem, as we will show in this report later.

The same construction applies to local observables. 
For an operator \(\hat O\) supported on one or a few sites, the expectation value is
\begin{equation}
O(t)=\langle \psi(t)|\hat O|\psi(t)\rangle=\langle \psi_0|U^\dagger(t)\hat O U(t)|\psi_0\rangle
\end{equation}
After Trotterizing both \(U(t)\) and \(U^\dagger(t)\), this becomes a double-layer spacetime tensor network, or equivalently a Keldysh contour, with a forward branch and a backward branch.
A direct transverse contraction is possible, but it is more efficient to fold the network in the time direction by pairing the forward and backward tensors at the same spatial location \cite{banulsMatrixProductStates2009b,hastingsConnectingEntanglementTime2015,Carignano2024TemporalEntropy}. 
After folding, each spatial column becomes a folded temporal MPO whose local physical dimension contains both the ket and bra time indices.
For sites away from the support of \(\hat O\), the folded column defines an environment temporal MPO \(E_i\). 
The column containing the local observable is modified and denoted by \(E_O\). 
Contracting all columns to the left gives a left temporal MPS \(\langle L|\), while contracting all columns to the right gives a right temporal MPS \(|R\rangle\). The local expectation value can then be written as
\begin{equation}
O(t)=\langle L|E_O|R\rangle .
\end{equation}
In this form, \(\langle L|\) and \(|R\rangle\) play the role of temporal environments, or influence-functional MPSs, for the local subsystem containing the observable. The local observable itself is treated as a small subsystem tensor \(E_O\), while the rest of the chain is compressed into the two temporal boundary MPSs.
